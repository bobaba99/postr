# SS3 — Does class size reduction improve student academic achievement?

*Field: social science. Fixture built from an Elicit systematic review ([review link](https://elicit.com/review/556b7120-f7ae-4dd2-839f-784325b5e6f7)) over 8 papers with full-text extraction.*

**Review summary.** Class size reduction produces small improvements in reading achievement but has no significant effect on mathematics, with effects typically too modest to justify the substantial implementation costs.

## Effects of Class Size Reduction

### Overall Effect Patterns

The evidence reveals substantial heterogeneity in findings. At one extreme, Hoxby found that class size does not have a statistically significant effect on student achievement (Hoxby, 2000), ruling out even modest effects of 2 to 4 percent of a standard deviation for a 10 percent reduction in class size (Hoxby, 2000). Similarly, Slavin reported that even substantial reductions in class size resulted in slight achievement differences, averaging only 13% of a standard deviation (Slavin, 1989), with meaningful effects not evident until class size approaches one (Slavin, 1989).

In contrast, Angrist and Lavy found that reductions in class size induce a significant increase in reading and math scores for 5th graders and a smaller increase in reading scores for 4th graders (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997). The Jepsen and Rivkin studies reported that smaller classes raised mathematics and reading achievement (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009), though these benefits were dampened by increases in teachers with neither prior experience nor full certification, particularly in schools with high shares of economically disadvantaged, minority students (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009).

The most comprehensive assessment, Filges et al.'s systematic review, concluded that the evidence suggests at best a small effect on reading achievement, with a 53 percent chance that a randomly selected score of a student from a small class exceeds that of a student from a larger class (Filges et al., 2018). For mathematics, there was no significant effect, with the average being negative though statistically insignificant (Filges et al., 2018).

The pattern of effects differs notably between reading and mathematics. While multiple studies report positive effects for both subjects, the systematic review by Filges et al. found a small positive effect for reading but a negative, albeit non-significant, effect for mathematics (Filges et al., 2018). This suggests that class size reduction may be more effective for reading instruction than mathematics, though the mechanisms underlying this difference remain unclear.

Grade level emerges as a critical moderator. The Angrist and Lavy studies consistently found larger effects for 5th graders than 4th graders, with little evidence of effects for 3rd graders (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997). Jepsen's review noted that when smaller classes are beneficial, they tend to be more beneficial for younger students than older students (Jepsen, 2015). However, the finding of no effect for 3rd graders while observing effects for 4th and 5th graders suggests the relationship is non-monotonic rather than simply stronger for younger students.

### Teacher Quality as a Moderating Factor

The California studies revealed a crucial confounding dynamic: the benefits of smaller classes were partially offset by increases in teachers with neither prior experience nor full certification, particularly affecting schools with high shares of economically disadvantaged, minority students (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009). This negative effect of changing teacher composition counterbalanced the positive direct effects of class size reduction, leading to neutral long-term impacts (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009).

The evidence on differential effects for disadvantaged students is contradictory. While Angrist and Lavy found that gains from small classes are largest for students from disadvantaged backgrounds (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997), the Jepsen and Rivkin studies showed that the negative effects of teacher quality changes were concentrated in schools serving economically disadvantaged, minority students (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009). This suggests that while disadvantaged students may benefit more from smaller classes per se, they are also more vulnerable to the negative consequences of policy-induced changes in teacher workforce composition.

## Synthesis

The heterogeneity in findings can be explained through several systematic patterns rather than reflecting simple measurement error or study quality differences.

**Study Design and Identification Strategy**

Studies using natural experiments with plausibly exogenous variation produced divergent results depending on their specific sources of variation. Angrist and Lavy's use of Maimonides' rule -- which creates sharp discontinuities in class size based on enrollment thresholds -- found significant positive effects for 5th graders (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997). This approach exploits rule-based variation that schools cannot manipulate. In contrast, Hoxby's use of idiosyncratic year-to-year population fluctuations -- variation that generates no evaluation or incentive effects -- found no statistically significant effects (Hoxby, 2000). Both studies employed credible identification strategies, but the Maimonides' rule approach may capture effects in contexts where class size assignments are more salient and persistent, while population-driven variation may represent more marginal, less stable changes.

**Grade-Specific Mechanisms**

The finding of significant effects for 5th graders but not 3rd graders (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997) contradicts the conventional wisdom that younger students benefit more from class size reduction (Jepsen, 2015). This non-monotonic pattern suggests that the relationship between class size and achievement depends on developmental stage in complex ways. Fifth grade may represent a developmental window where reduced class size enables instructional approaches or peer interactions that are particularly consequential for standardized test performance, whereas 3rd graders may not yet be able to capitalize on these opportunities or their learning may be less sensitive to class size at that stage.

**Teacher Labor Market Effects**

The California studies revealed a critical equilibrium effect: large-scale class size reduction policies trigger teacher labor market responses that partially offset direct benefits. When California mandated smaller classes, schools hired teachers with neither prior experience nor full certification, and this compositional change dampened achievement gains, particularly in disadvantaged schools (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009). Schools serving more advantaged populations likely had greater capacity to attract qualified teachers even during expansion. This explains why small-scale experiments or studies in contexts with stable teacher supply might find larger effects than large-scale policy implementations. The effect observed in any particular study thus depends on whether the class size variation occurs within a stable teacher workforce or triggers labor market responses.

**Subject-Specific Instructional Mechanisms**

The divergence between reading and mathematics effects (Filges et al., 2018) suggests that smaller classes may facilitate certain instructional approaches more than others. Reading instruction, particularly in elementary grades, often involves individualized attention to decoding and comprehension skills, which may benefit from reduced student-teacher ratios. Mathematics instruction may be more amenable to whole-class direct instruction where class size is less constraining, or may require specialized pedagogical knowledge that is less available when class size reduction depletes the qualified teacher pool. The negative, though non-significant, mathematics effect in Filges et al.'s meta-analysis (Filges et al., 2018) raises the possibility that in some contexts, hastily implemented class size reduction could actually harm mathematics learning if it leads to less qualified mathematics instruction.

**Magnitude and Cost-Effectiveness Considerations**

Even when positive effects are found, their magnitude is consistently modest. Slavin reported average effects of only 13% of a standard deviation (Slavin, 1989), and Filges et al. characterized the reading effect as translating to a 53% probability of superiority -- barely above chance (Filges et al., 2018). Jepsen concluded that reducing class size leads to only modest improvements in most cases (Jepsen, 2015). These small effect sizes must be weighed against the substantial costs of class size reduction, which requires hiring more teachers and building more classrooms. The null findings from Hoxby (Hoxby, 2000) and the modest effects from other studies suggest that when teacher quality is held constant and variation is truly exogenous to school and family choices, the direct effect of class size may be smaller than typically assumed.

**Reconciliation of Contradictory Evidence**

The evidence can be reconciled as follows: Class size reduction likely has a small positive direct effect on reading achievement in elementary grades, particularly for 4th and 5th graders (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997), but this effect is often negated by labor market responses when implemented at scale (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009). The effect on mathematics is near zero or possibly negative (Filges et al., 2018). Students from disadvantaged backgrounds may benefit more from the direct effects of smaller classes (Angrist & Lavy, 1999; Angrist & Lavy, 1997), but are also more exposed to the negative consequences of teacher quality degradation in large-scale implementations (Jepsen & Rivkin, 2012; Jepsen & Rivkin, 2009). The effect is not observable when class size variation is highly marginal and unaccompanied by evaluation effects (Hoxby, 2000), suggesting that either the mechanism requires more substantial and sustained class size differences or that awareness and instructional response play a role. The overall impact is sufficiently small that even with 127 studies, the meta-analytic estimate remains near the threshold of practical significance (Filges et al., 2018).

> "Reducing class size leads to, in most cases, only modest improvements in student achievement. In general, smaller classes are associated with increased student achievement, usually measured by standardized tests in multiple subjects such as mathematics and reading.
