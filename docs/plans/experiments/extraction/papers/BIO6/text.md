# BIO6 — Does ocean warming reduce coral reef calcification?

## Results

### Characteristics of Included Studies

Eight studies examining the effects of ocean warming on calcification in various coral reef organisms were included. Full text was available for five studies, while three were assessed based on abstracts only.

The studies examined diverse calcifying organisms including scleractinian corals, calcareous macroalgae, coralline algae, and foraminifera. Four studies focused on single coral species, two examined calcareous algae, one conducted a meta-analysis of coralline algae, and two were modeling studies of coral reef calcification.

### Effects of Ocean Warming on Calcification

The effects of ocean warming on calcification varied substantially across studies, organisms, and temperature ranges tested. Three primary patterns emerged: enhanced calcification at moderate warming, reduced calcification at high temperatures, and species-specific responses.

#### Enhanced Calcification at Moderate Warming

Two modeling studies predicted net increases in calcification with future ocean warming. McNeil et al. projected that annual average coral reef calcification rates would increase with future warming and eventually exceed pre-industrial rates by approximately 35% by 2100[B. McNeil et al., 2004]. Their analysis incorporated both temperature and carbonate chemistry effects, concluding that present calcification rates are equivalent to late 19th century levels and do not support predictions of large future decreases[B. McNeil et al., 2004].

Castillo et al. observed parabolic responses to warming in *Siderastrea siderea*, where moderate temperature increases relative to near-present-day values enhanced coral calcification[K. Castillo et al., 2014]. However, calcification rates declined under the highest thermal conditions tested[K. Castillo et al., 2014]. Similarly, Yang et al. found positive effects on algal growth rate and chlorophyll content when temperature increased from 26 to 28°C, though these parameters decreased significantly at 32°C[Fangfang Yang et al., 2021].

#### Reduced Calcification at Elevated Temperatures

Most experimental studies demonstrated negative effects of warming on calcification when temperatures exceeded species-specific thresholds. Yang et al. reported that calcification rate in *Amphiroa fragilissima* was inhibited significantly at 32 and 34°C, despite no significant differences in CaCO₃ content across temperature treatments[Fangfang Yang et al., 2021].

Ross et al. found that temperature and light effects on coral physiology and calcification mechanisms are interactive and context-specific, with prolonged exposure to high temperatures causing heat stress and heightened sensitivity to light under static conditions[Claire L. Ross et al., 2022]. Sinutok et al. documented that elevated temperature treatments greatly reduced calcification in *Marginopora vertebralis* and *Halimeda* species, with calcification rates significantly reduced at 34°C across all pH levels[S. Sinutok et al., 2011].

Cornwall et al.'s meta-analysis of 40 responses from 14 studies found a net negative impact of increasing temperature on coralline algal calcification at 5.2°C above ambient conditions[C. Cornwall et al., 2019]. The analysis also revealed negative effects when temperature dropped below 2.0°C from ambient conditions[C. Cornwall et al., 2019].

Castillo et al. reported that warming projected by the IPCC for the end of the twenty-first century caused a fivefold decrease in calcification rate in *S. siderea*, while projected acidification had no statistically significant impact, suggesting that ocean warming poses a more immediate threat than acidification for this species[K. Castillo et al., 2014].

#### Species-Specific Responses

Substantial variation in thermal sensitivity was evident across coral species. Schoepf et al. found that after one month under experimental conditions, only *Acropora millepora* decreased calcification (−53%) in response to seawater pCO₂ expected by the end of this century, whereas the other three species tested (*Montipora monasteriata*, *Pocillopora damicornis*, *Turbinaria reniformis*) maintained calcification rates even when both pCO₂ and temperature were elevated[V. Schoepf et al., 2013].

The differential responses among coral species suggest that taxonomic identity and potentially evolutionary history influence thermal tolerance and calcification resilience.

#### Interaction with Experimental Design

The duration and pattern of thermal exposure substantially influenced outcomes. Ross et al. demonstrated that under seasonal temperature regimes, coral calcification rates responded dynamically to changes in both temperature and light, but under static conditions, artificially prolonged exposure to summer temperatures resulted in heat stress and heightened sensitivity to light[Claire L. Ross et al., 2022]. This finding emphasizes that experimental design—specifically whether temperature varies naturally or remains elevated—affects the observed calcification response.

Evenhuis et al.'s modeling work highlighted the strong dependence of calcification on temperature, showing that calcification rates across a range of aragonite saturation states are reduced at temperatures above 29°C and below 21°C relative to an optimal temperature of approximately 25°C[C. Evenhuis et al., 2014].

### Synthesis

The apparent contradiction between studies predicting enhanced calcification with warming and those demonstrating reduced calcification can be reconciled by examining temperature ranges, experimental durations, organism types, and methodological approaches.

#### Temperature Thresholds and Non-Linear Responses

The relationship between temperature and calcification is non-linear, exhibiting a parabolic or threshold pattern rather than a simple linear decline. Studies reporting enhanced calcification examined moderate warming (1-3°C above current levels), while studies documenting reduced calcification tested more extreme temperature increases (5-8°C above ambient) or temperatures approaching species thermal maxima.

McNeil et al.'s projection of 35% increased calcification by 2100[B. McNeil et al., 2004] incorporated future warming scenarios that may fall within the optimal range for many coral reef organisms. In contrast, experimental studies testing temperatures of 32-34°C consistently observed negative effects[Fangfang Yang et al., 2021; S. Sinutok et al., 2011], as these temperatures exceed thermal optima. Castillo et al.'s finding that *S. siderea* exhibited enhanced calcification at moderate warming but experienced an 80% decline at 32°C[K. Castillo et al., 2014] exemplifies this threshold behavior. Similarly, Cornwall et al. found no significant negative effect until temperatures exceeded ambient by 5.2°C[C. Cornwall et al., 2019].

This threshold pattern aligns with known physiological mechanisms: calcification and photosynthesis are enzyme-mediated processes with temperature optima, and performance declines when thermal stress denatures proteins or disrupts cellular function.

#### Duration of Exposure and Acclimation

Studies differed substantially in exposure duration, ranging from weeks to projected century-scale changes, which may explain divergent findings. Ross et al.'s experiment directly demonstrated that response to warming depends on exposure pattern: under naturally varying seasonal temperatures, corals maintained calcification, but prolonged static exposure to the same high temperatures caused heat stress and reduced calcification[Claire L. Ross et al., 2022].

Short-term experiments (3-5 weeks) may capture initial stress responses but miss longer-term acclimation or energetic depletion. Schoepf et al.'s one-month experiment found that three of four species maintained calcification[V. Schoepf et al., 2013], potentially because feeding every two weeks supplemented energy reserves. The study explicitly noted that coral energy reserves were generally not metabolized to sustain calcification under experimental conditions[V. Schoepf et al., 2013], suggesting that longer-term experiments without feeding might reveal different outcomes.

Century-scale projections like McNeil et al.'s incorporate assumptions about coral adaptation and migration that experimental studies cannot test. Their model assumes corals can track optimal thermal conditions or adapt to gradual warming, while experimental studies test acute responses to fixed temperature increases.

#### Species and Taxonomic Differences

Substantial taxonomic variation in thermal sensitivity emerges across studies. Among scleractinian corals, *Acropora millepora* exhibited a 53% decline in calcification[V. Schoepf et al., 2013], while other coral species in the same experiment maintained rates[V. Schoepf et al., 2013]. This suggests that branching Acroporid corals may be more thermally sensitive than massive or other morphologies.

Calcareous algae and foraminifera appear particularly vulnerable to warming. Sinutok et al. found that *Halimeda* species and *Marginopora vertebralis* all experienced greatly reduced calcification and died at 34°C under all CO₂ treatments[S. Sinutok et al., 2011]. Yang et al. reported significant inhibition of calcification in *Amphiroa fragilissima* at 32°C[Fangfang Yang et al., 2021]. These calcifying algae and foraminifera may have narrower thermal tolerance windows than scleractinian corals.

The meta-analysis by Cornwall et al. found that coralline algae show net negative impacts at +5.2°C[C. Cornwall et al., 2019], suggesting this group is relatively robust compared to some individual species but still exhibits thermal limits. Notably, Cornwall et al. proposed that species with short generation times and high phenotypic plasticity may be more capable of acclimatizing and adapting to warming[C. Cornwall et al., 2019], potentially explaining why some taxa are more resilient.

#### Methodological Considerations and Study Design

The two modeling studies that predicted enhanced calcification[B. McNeil et al., 2004; C. Evenhuis et al., 2014] differ fundamentally from experimental studies in their approach and assumptions. McNeil et al. combined empirical relationships between calcification rate and both temperature and carbonate saturation with climate model output[B. McNeil et al., 2004], but these empirical relationships were likely derived from present-day spatial gradients rather than temporal warming experiments. Spatial correlations between temperature and calcification (higher calcification in warmer regions) may reflect adapted populations rather than plastic responses, making them poor predictors of future warming impacts on existing populations.

Evenhuis et al.'s model incorporated Arrhenius kinetics and thermal specialization principles[C. Evenhuis et al., 2014], which predict enhanced calcification with moderate warming based on metabolic theory. However, the model may not fully capture physiological stress responses, bleaching, or mortality that occur in experimental studies at high temperatures.

In contrast, experimental studies directly manipulated temperature and measured calcification over weeks to months, providing direct evidence of physiological responses but potentially missing longer-term adaptation. The fact that experimental studies consistently find negative effects at high temperatures (32-34°C), while models predict benefits, suggests that models may inadequately represent thermal stress mechanisms or overestimate adaptation potential.

#### Interactions with Light and Carbonate Chemistry

Ocean warming does not occur in isolation, and interactions with other environmental factors influence calcification responses. Ross et al. demonstrated that temperature and light effects are interactive and context-specific, with prolonged high temperatures increasing sensitivity to light stress[Claire L. Ross et al., 2022]. This suggests that predictions based solely on temperature may underestimate negative impacts if light stress co-occurs.

Several studies examined temperature effects in combination with ocean acidification. Schoepf et al. found that temperature often modulated the response to acidification, both mitigating and worsening pCO₂ effects depending on the species and metric examined[V. Schoepf et al., 2013]. Castillo et al.'s finding that projected warming caused larger calcification declines than projected acidification[K. Castillo et al., 2014] contrasts with Schoepf et al.'s observation that warming did not exacerbate acidification effects in most species[V. Schoepf et al., 2013], suggesting complex interactions that vary by species and warming magnitude.

Sinutok et al. found that elevated CO₂ and temperature together greatly reduced calcification[S. Sinutok et al., 2011], demonstrating synergistic effects. The combined stress of multiple factors may explain why field observations sometimes show greater impacts than single-stressor experiments predict.

#### Reconciling Findings Across Temperature Ranges

The evidence supports the following synthesis: moderate ocean warming (1-3°C above current temperatures) may maintain or modestly enhance calcification in some coral species, particularly those not already living near their thermal maxima. However, warming beyond approximately 4-5°C above ambient, or warming that brings temperatures above 30-32°C, consistently reduces calcification across coral reef organisms. The effect becomes more pronounced with longer exposure durations, in species with narrow thermal tolerances (particularly some calcareous algae and foraminifera), and when warming co-occurs with other stressors like high light or ocean acidification.

McNeil et al.'s projection of increased calcification[B. McNeil et al., 2004] may apply if future warming remains within the 1-3°C range and if corals can adapt or migrate to track optimal conditions, but experimental evidence suggests this optimistic scenario is unlikely for temperature increases exceeding 4-5°C or for thermally sensitive species. For most coral reef ecosystems experiencing projected end-century warming, the preponderance of experimental evidence indicates reduced calcification, with particularly severe impacts expected in regions where present temperatures already approach thermal maxima.
