The studies varied in their access to full text, with four providing complete manuscripts and four limited to abstracts. The methodological approaches ranged from conceptual analyses of causality[Isaac T. W. Harley & C. Karp, 2012] to systematic reviews of animal model evidence[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015; P. ## Evidence for Causal Relationships

### Animal Model Evidence

Multiple studies reported evidence from animal models supporting a causal role for gut microbiota in obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Studies in germ-free mice demonstrated protection against diet-induced obesity compared to conventionally raised mice[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015]. The use of germ-free animals and microbiota transplant methods showed that some gut microbiota could confer obesity and associated metabolic disorders to mice, suggesting a causative link between gut bacteria and metabolic diseases[P. Gerard, 2017]. Specifically, conventionalization studies, studies in germ-free mice, and studies using antibiotics provided evidence that changes in gut microbiota may be causally involved in metabolic disease, rather than merely being a consequence of it[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015].

The ability of altered gut microbiome composition to induce obesity in germ-free mice[Kexin Zhang et al., 2024] demonstrated that microbiome changes are sufficient to drive metabolic dysfunction in controlled experimental settings. These animal studies consistently supported the hypothesis that gut microbiota alterations can cause obesity[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015; P. Gerard, 2017].

### Human Association Studies

Human studies revealed consistent associations between reduced gut microbiome diversity and metabolic dysfunction, though establishing causality remained challenging. Recent data highlighted the role of gut dysbiosis in the etiology and pathogenesis of metabolic disorders, including obesity, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease[N. Vallianou et al., 2019]. Most studies demonstrated a reduction in gut microbiome diversity and richness in obese subjects, with reduced gut microbiome diversity associated with an elevated relative risk of obesity and metabolic disorders[N. Vallianou et al., 2019].

Studies suggested that obesity is related to low microbial diversity and taxon depletion, sometimes resulting from interaction with host dietary habits and genotype[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017]. These findings support the idea that the depletion or absence of certain taxa leaves an empty niche, likely leading to compromised functionality and thus promoting dysbiosis[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017]. Certain alterations in the microbiota structure in the gut were discovered to be linked with metabolic conditions, especially insulin resistance and inadequate body fat distribution[L. Fang, 2026].

Obesity has been shown to cause alterations in the gut microbiome, including a reduction in bacterial diversity[Kexin Zhang et al., 2024], though the directionality of this relationship remained a subject of investigation across the literature. Human studies generally supported the hypothesis that reduced diversity is associated with metabolic dysfunction[L. Fang, 2026; S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017; N. Vallianou et al., 2019; Kexin Zhang et al., 2024], though evidence quality varied.

### Mechanistic Insights

The reviews identified several potential mechanisms linking gut microbiota alterations to metabolic dysfunction. Changes in gut microbial composition may be unfavorable and predispose an individual to disease[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015]. The functional output of microbial metabolism, particularly the conversion of dietary components into bioactive products that control host inflammatory responses and insulin sensitivity, appeared critical[L. Fang, 2026]. The gut microbiome's role in energy absorption and distribution, influence on bile acid synthesis, and production of short-chain fatty acids provided plausible biological pathways through which reduced diversity could contribute to metabolic dysfunction[Kexin Zhang et al., 2024].

## Synthesis

The evidence base reveals a significant gap between strong causal evidence in animal models and predominantly associational evidence in humans. While animal studies using germ-free mice and microbiota transplantation clearly demonstrate that gut microbiota alterations can cause obesity and metabolic disorders[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015; P. Gerard, 2017], human evidence showing causality remains scarce[Abraham S. Meijnikman et al., 2018]. This discrepancy reflects fundamental methodological challenges rather than contradictory findings.

The quality of evidence hierarchy weighs heavily toward animal studies for establishing causation. Animal models allow for controlled interventions that establish directionality—germ-free mice can be colonized with specific microbial communities and metabolic outcomes observed[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015; P. Gerard, 2017]. Human studies, by contrast, typically observe correlations between existing microbiome states and metabolic conditions without the ability to manipulate microbiota composition in controlled ways[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017; N. Vallianou et al., 2019]. The consistent finding that obesity is associated with low microbial diversity and taxon depletion[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017] does not definitively establish whether reduced diversity causes obesity, results from obesity, or reflects a third factor influencing both.

Several mechanistic pathways provide biological plausibility for causation. The depletion of certain taxa may leave functional niches empty, leading to compromised metabolic functions and promoting dysbiosis[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017]. Alterations in microbiota structure appear linked to metabolic conditions through the functional output of microbial metabolism—specifically, the conversion of dietary components into bioactive products that influence host inflammatory responses and insulin sensitivity[L. Fang, 2026]. The gut microbiome's roles in energy extraction, bile acid synthesis, and short-chain fatty acid production provide concrete mechanisms through which reduced diversity could impair metabolic regulation[Kexin Zhang et al., 2024].

The bidirectional nature of the relationship complicates causal inference. Obesity causes alterations in the gut microbiome, including reductions in bacterial diversity[Kexin Zhang et al., 2024], suggesting that some observed associations may reflect consequences rather than causes. The interaction with dietary habits and host genotype[S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017] indicates that microbiome alterations, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction exist within a complex causal network rather than a simple linear pathway.

Based on the available evidence, the most defensible conclusion is that gut microbiota alterations can causally contribute to obesity and metabolic dysfunction in animal models under controlled conditions[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015; P. Gerard, 2017], while human evidence consistently demonstrates associations between reduced diversity and metabolic disorders[L. Fang, 2026; S. Moran-Ramos et al., 2017; N. Vallianou et al., 2019; Kexin Zhang et al., 2024] without definitively establishing causation. The biological mechanisms are plausible and well-characterized[L. Fang, 2026; Kexin Zhang et al., 2024], but the epistemological challenges of proving causality in humans remain unresolved[Isaac T. W. Harley & C. Karp, 2012; Abraham S. Meijnikman et al., 2018]. More experimental evidence from human intervention studies manipulating gut microbiota composition would be necessary to establish causal relationships with the same confidence achieved in animal models[A. Janssen & S. Kersten, 2015].
