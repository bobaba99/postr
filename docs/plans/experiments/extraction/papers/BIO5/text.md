# BIO5 — Does amyloid-beta accumulation drive cognitive decline in Alzheimer disease?

> Synthesis conclusion: Amyloid-beta accumulation drives cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease, particularly through soluble oligomeric and intraneuronal forms that act as early initiating factors triggering synaptic dysfunction and multiple downstream pathological cascades.

# Results

## Characteristics of included studies

The review included eight studies published between 2000 and 2022, all examining the relationship between amyloid-beta accumulation and cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease. Full texts were retrieved for six studies[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; Y. Jung et al., 2013; D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022; Ernesto Solis et al., 2020], while two studies were available as abstracts only[J. Näslund et al., 2000; L. Billings et al., 2005].

| Study | Full text retrieved? | Study type | Primary research focus | Effect direction |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| Reddy & Beal, 2008 | Yes | Review | Aβ effects on mitochondrial function and synaptic damage | Supports |
| Näslund et al., 2000 | No | Cross-sectional postmortem study | Correlation between Aβ levels and dementia severity | Supports |
| Findley et al., 2019 | Yes | Review | Aβ-mediated alterations to glutamate signaling | Supports |
| Jung et al., 2013 | Yes | Observational study | Aβ burden in AD patients with aphasia | Supports |
| Selkoe & Hardy, 2016 | Yes | Review | Amyloid hypothesis at 25 years | Supports |
| Billings et al., 2005 | No | Animal model study | Intraneuronal Aβ and cognitive deficits in transgenic mice | Supports |
| Roda et al., 2022 | Yes | Review | Aβ and tau crosstalk | Contradicts |
| Solis et al., 2020 | Yes | Review | Aβ and neurovascular dysfunction | Supports |

Seven of eight studies supported the hypothesis that amyloid-beta accumulation drives cognitive decline[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; J. Näslund et al., 2000; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; Y. Jung et al., 2013; D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; L. Billings et al., 2005; Ernesto Solis et al., 2020], while one study challenged this view[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]. The studies employed diverse methodological approaches including postmortem human brain analysis, animal models, PET imaging, and mechanistic reviews.

## Effects of amyloid-beta on cognitive decline

### Temporal relationship and correlation strength

Multiple studies demonstrated that amyloid-beta accumulation precedes or coincides with cognitive impairment. Näslund et al. found that Aβ levels were elevated even in cases with questionable dementia (CDR score = 0.5), and increases in both Aβx-40 and Aβx-42 strongly correlated with dementia progression[J. Näslund et al., 2000]. Billings et al. identified the earliest cognitive impairment at 4 months in transgenic mice, which manifested as a deficit in long-term retention and correlated with intraneuronal Aβ accumulation in the hippocampus and amygdala[L. Billings et al., 2005]. Jung et al. found a significant inverse correlation between global PiB ratio and performance on cognitive tests (MoCA: β = -12.33, p = 0.02; WMS-III VR I: β = -2.38, p = 0.02; VR II: β = -1.85, p = 0.04)[Y. Jung et al., 2013].

| Finding | Supporting studies | Key evidence |
|----|----|----|
| Aβ elevation precedes cognitive symptoms | Näslund et al., 2000 | Aβ elevated in questionable dementia (CDR 0.5) |
| Aβ correlates with cognitive decline | Näslund et al., 2000<br>Jung et al., 2013 | Strong correlation between Aβ levels and dementia progression<br>Inverse correlation: higher PiB ratio, worse cognition (p = 0.02) |
| Intraneuronal Aβ drives early deficits | Billings et al., 2005 | Cognitive impairment at 4 months correlates with intraneuronal Aβ |
| Aβ as initiating factor | Selkoe & Hardy, 2016 | Aβ42 imbalance is "very early, often initiating factor" in AD |

The correlation between amyloid burden and cognitive decline was particularly evident when measured using sensitive biomarkers. Jung et al. demonstrated that increased cortical β-amyloid burden measured by PiB-PET was associated with worse cognition in AD patients with language impairment, suggesting that amyloid burden contributes to cognitive impairment independent of primary presenting symptoms[Y. Jung et al., 2013].

### Mechanistic pathways

Studies identified multiple mechanisms through which amyloid-beta may drive cognitive decline:

| Mechanism | Pathway description | Key molecules/processes | Supporting studies |
|----|----|----|----|
| Synaptic dysfunction | Aβ accumulation at synaptic terminals causes synaptic damage | Soluble oligomeric Aβ disrupts synaptic plasticity | Reddy & Beal, 2008 |
| Mitochondrial impairment | Aβ localizes to mitochondrial membranes and disrupts function | Electron transport chain disruption, increased ROS production | Reddy & Beal, 2008 |
| Glutamatergic dysregulation | Soluble Aβ accumulation depresses long-term potentiation | Biphasic glutamate signaling changes: hyperactivity then hypoactivity | Findley et al., 2019 |
| Neurovascular dysfunction | Vascular abnormalities linked to Aβ accumulation | Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, blood-brain barrier deterioration | Solis et al., 2020 |

Findley et al. described how soluble Aβ accumulation results in depression of long-term potentiation leading to cognitive deficits characteristic of AD[C. A. Findley et al., 2019]. The review emphasized that the glutamatergic hypoactivation observed in later disease stages may be the consequence of preceding hyperglutamatergic activity[C. A. Findley et al., 2019].

### Reversibility evidence

Billings et al. provided critical evidence for causality through immunotherapy experiments. Clearance of intraneuronal Aβ pathology by immunotherapy rescued early cognitive deficits in transgenic mice, and reemergence of Aβ pathology led to return of cognitive deficits[L. Billings et al., 2005]. This bidirectional relationship strongly implicates intraneuronal Aβ as a driver rather than merely a correlate of cognitive dysfunction[L. Billings et al., 2005].

Selkoe and Hardy noted that recent trials of three different Aβ antibodies (solanezumab, crenezumab, and aducanumab) suggested slowing of cognitive decline in post hoc analyses of mild AD subjects[D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016], further supporting a causal role for Aβ in cognitive decline.

## Synthesis

The evidence presents an apparent paradox: seven studies support amyloid-beta as a driver of cognitive decline[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; J. Näslund et al., 2000; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; Y. Jung et al., 2013; D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; L. Billings et al., 2005; Ernesto Solis et al., 2020], yet Roda et al. challenges this conclusion, noting the poor correlation between amyloid plaques and cognitive decline[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]. This heterogeneity can be reconciled through several key distinctions.

### Form-specific effects of amyloid-beta

The conflicting findings largely dissolve when distinguishing between different forms of amyloid-beta. Studies supporting the amyloid hypothesis focus on soluble Aβ oligomers and intraneuronal Aβ accumulation[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; L. Billings et al., 2005], while the criticism by Roda et al. specifically addresses the poor correlation between insoluble amyloid plaques and cognitive decline[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]. Reddy and Beal emphasized that soluble oligomeric Aβ disrupts synaptic plasticity[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008], and Findley et al. explicitly stated that "soluble Aβ, rather than insoluble plaques, may instigate synaptic failure"[C. A. Findley et al., 2019]. Selkoe and Hardy noted that soluble oligomers of Aβ42 isolated from AD patients' brains can impair synaptic function and memory in rodents[D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016].

This distinction is critical: the poor correlation with plaques does not negate the role of soluble Aβ species. Indeed, Billings et al. found that cognitive impairment occurred at 4 months when intraneuronal Aβ was present but before plaque or tangle pathology was apparent[L. Billings et al., 2005], suggesting that soluble and intracellular forms of Aβ drive early cognitive changes.

### Amyloid-beta as initiator versus effector

Roda et al.'s framework helps reconcile the evidence by proposing that "Aβ is the 'trigger' and tau the 'bullet' driving AD"[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]. This model suggests that both perspectives may be correct at different disease stages. Amyloid-beta accumulation serves as an early initiating factor[D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016] that sets in motion a cascade including tau hyperphosphorylation[D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]. The stronger correlation between tau tangles and cognitive symptoms in later disease stages[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022] does not invalidate Aβ's role in early cognitive changes, as demonstrated by studies showing Aβ elevation in questionable dementia before significant tau pathology[J. Näslund et al., 2000].

Selkoe and Hardy's evidence that "increases in Aβx-40 and Aβx-42 precede significant tau pathology at least in the frontal cortex"[J. Näslund et al., 2000] supports this temporal sequence where Aβ initiates pathological processes that subsequently involve tau-mediated neurodegeneration.

### Multiple pathways to cognitive decline

The studies reveal that Aβ likely contributes to cognitive decline through multiple converging mechanisms rather than a single pathway. The mechanistic evidence spans synaptic dysfunction[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008], mitochondrial impairment[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008], glutamatergic dysregulation[C. A. Findley et al., 2019], and neurovascular dysfunction[Ernesto Solis et al., 2020]. Solis et al. noted that vascular dysfunction may precede Aβ accumulation[Ernesto Solis et al., 2020], suggesting bidirectional relationships where vascular pathology can exacerbate Aβ accumulation, which in turn worsens vascular function—creating a feed-forward cycle.

This multi-pathway model explains why targeting only one mechanism (such as plaque removal) may show limited clinical benefit while Aβ reduction through other means shows promise: the relevant therapeutic window may be early in disease when soluble Aβ species are driving synaptic and mitochondrial dysfunction, before extensive plaque formation and tau pathology have occurred.

### Implications for interpretation

The synthesis indicates that amyloid-beta accumulation does drive cognitive decline in AD, but with important qualifications:

- The effect is strongest for soluble Aβ oligomers and intraneuronal Aβ, not insoluble plaques[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; L. Billings et al., 2005]
- The relationship is most direct in early disease stages before extensive tau pathology[J. Näslund et al., 2000; L. Billings et al., 2005]
- Multiple downstream mechanisms mediate the effect, including synaptic, mitochondrial, glutamatergic, and vascular pathways[P. Reddy & M. Beal, 2008; C. A. Findley et al., 2019; Ernesto Solis et al., 2020]
- Aβ acts as an initiating factor that triggers cascades involving tau and other pathologies[D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022]

The apparently contradictory finding by Roda et al.[Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022] actually complements rather than refutes the supporting evidence, highlighting that crude measures of total amyloid burden (particularly plaques) inadequately capture the dynamic role of soluble Aβ species in early cognitive decline. The evidence collectively supports targeting Aβ accumulation, particularly soluble forms, as a strategy to prevent or delay cognitive decline when initiated early in the disease process[J. Näslund et al., 2000; D. Selkoe & J. Hardy, 2016; L. Billings et al., 2005].

## Per-paper findings

The following findings were extracted from the individual studies included in this review. Each supporting quote is reproduced verbatim from the source paper.

### Ernesto Solis et al., 2020 — supports the hypothesis

Neurovascular dysfunction is causally linked to the development of Alzheimer's disease, often preceding amyloid-β accumulation and contributing to cognitive decline.

> "The eventual neuronal dysfunction and cell death associated with this cascade of events leads to widespread cerebral atrophy that is prominent throughout the neocortex including the medial temporal lobe, cingulate gyrus, parietal lobe, and frontal lobe."

[Alzheimer's Disease: The Link Between Amyloid-β and Neurovascular Dysfunction.](https://doi.org/10.3233/jad-200473)

### P. Reddy et al., 2008 — supports the hypothesis

Amyloid-beta accumulation at synaptic terminals contributes to synaptic damage and cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease by impairing synaptic function and causing mitochondrial damage.

> "Furthermore, accumulation of Abeta at synaptic terminals might contribute to synaptic damage and cognitive decline in patients with AD."

[Amyloid beta, mitochondrial dysfunction and synaptic damage: implications for cognitive decline in aging and Alzheimer's disease.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2007.12.002)

### Alejandro R Roda et al., 2022 — contradicts the hypothesis

The main finding is that while amyloid-beta accumulation may initiate the cascade of events in Alzheimer's disease, it is tau impairment that is likely the effector molecule of neurodegeneration, suggesting that tau is more directly linked to cognitive decline than amyloid-beta.

> "The poor correlation found between cognitive decline and amyloid plaques, have led the scientific community to question whether amyloid-beta accumulation is actually triggering neurodegeneration in Alzheimer’s disease. The occurrence of tau neurofibrillary tangles better correlates to neuronal loss and clinical symptoms and, although amyloid-beta may initiate the cascade of events, tau impairment is likely the effector molecule of neurodegeneration. Currently it is becoming widely accepted that Aβ is the 'trigger' and tau the 'bullet' driving AD. The fact that Aβ-immunotherapy still raises some concerns is in agreement with the new idea of Aβ acting just as a switch-on. Once Aβ triggers the cascade of events that leads to neuronal death and dementia, it is not that important whether its levels can be reduced or not because the effector molecule driving to synaptic impairment and neuronal death is tau."

[Amyloid-beta peptide and tau protein crosstalk in Alzheimer’s disease](https://doi.org/10.4103/1673-5374.332127)

### C. A. Findley et al., 2019 — supports the hypothesis

Soluble amyloid-beta accumulation drives cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease by modulating the glutamatergic system, leading to synaptic failure and depression of long-term potentiation.

> "Soluble Aβ accumulation results in depression of long-term potentiation leading to cognitive deficits commonly characterized in AD."

[Amyloid Beta-Related Alterations to Glutamate Signaling Dynamics During Alzheimer’s Disease Progression](https://doi.org/10.1177/1759091419855541)

### D. Selkoe et al., 2016 — supports the hypothesis

Amyloid-beta accumulation, particularly through soluble oligomers of Aβ42, drives cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease by impairing synaptic function and inducing tau pathology, as supported by clinical trials with Aβ antibodies showing a slowing of cognitive decline.

> "Despite continuing debate about the amyloid β‐protein (or Aβ hypothesis, new lines of evidence from laboratories and clinics worldwide support the concept that an imbalance between production and clearance of Aβ42 and related Aβ peptides is a very early, often initiating factor in Alzheimer's disease (AD)."

[The amyloid hypothesis of Alzheimer's disease at 25 years](https://doi.org/10.15252/emmm.201606210)

### L. Billings et al., 2005 — supports the hypothesis

The accumulation of intraneuronal Abeta in the hippocampus and amygdala correlates with the onset of cognitive dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease, as evidenced by the rescue of cognitive deficits upon clearance of Abeta pathology and their reemergence with its return.

> "The earliest cognitive impairment manifests at 4 months as a deficit in long-term retention and correlates with the accumulation of intraneuronal Abeta in the hippocampus and amygdala."

[Intraneuronal Aβ Causes the Onset of Early Alzheimer’s Disease-Related Cognitive Deficits in Transgenic Mice](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2005.01.040)

### J. Näslund et al., 2000 — supports the hypothesis

The levels of amyloid beta-peptide (Abeta) are elevated early in dementia and are strongly correlated with cognitive decline, suggesting that Abeta accumulation drives cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease.

> "In this study, levels of total A(beta)x-40 and A(beta)x-42 were elevated early in dementia and levels of both peptides were strongly correlated with cognitive decline. These results support an important role for Abeta in mediating initial pathogenic events in AD dementia and suggest that treatment strategies targeting the formation, accumulation, or cytotoxic effects of Abeta should be pursued."

[Correlation between elevated levels of amyloid beta-peptide in the brain and cognitive decline.](https://doi.org/10.1001/JAMA.283.12.1571)

### Y. Jung et al., 2013 — supports the hypothesis

Increased cortical β-amyloid burden is associated with worse cognition in Alzheimer's disease patients presenting with early and prominent language impairment.

> "The present study demonstrates that increased cortical β-amyloid burden measured by [ 11 C] PiB-PET is associated with worse cognition in patients with AD presenting with early and prominent language impairment. These findings, along with the results of our study, suggest that amyloid burden may be in part responsible for cognitive impairment in patients with AD, independent of the primary presenting symptoms. There was a significant inverse correlation between the global PiB ratio and performance on the MoCA (β = -12.33, t(34) = -2.48, p = 0.02) and the WMS-III VR (VR I z-score, β = -2.38, t(32) = -2.45, p = 0.02; VR II z-score, β = -1.85, t(32) = -2.09, p = 0.04)."

[Amyloid Burden Correlates with Cognitive Decline in Alzheimer’s Disease Presenting with Aphasia](https://doi.org/10.1111/ene.12331)
