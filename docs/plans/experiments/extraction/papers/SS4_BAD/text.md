## Introduction

More than half of humanity now lives in cities, and this share is projected to keep climbing over the coming decades. Rapid urbanization has reshaped the physical environment in which people spend their lives: dense housing, expanding road networks, and shrinking distances between industry and residence. A large body of environmental-health research has examined how these urban conditions affect the body. Traffic-related air pollution, ambient particulate matter, and elevated noise exposure have all been tied to cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, and premature mortality. The heat-island effect concentrates warmth in built-up areas, compounding cardiometabolic strain during summer months. Understanding how the fabric of the city shapes population-level physical health remains a central concern of urban epidemiology and public-health planning.

Alongside these environmental exposures, the way cities are designed profoundly influences human behavior, and in particular physical activity. Neighborhoods that are walkable, well-connected, and served by accessible recreation infrastructure tend to support higher levels of habitual movement, whereas car-dependent, sprawling development is associated with sedentary lifestyles. Because physical inactivity is a leading modifiable risk factor for obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, planners and epidemiologists have devoted considerable attention to the features of the built environment that encourage or discourage active living. Parks, trails, and open recreational areas figure prominently in this literature as venues that can raise activity levels across the lifespan, from childhood through older age.

Urban vegetation is also studied from an entirely ecological standpoint, quite apart from any effect on residents' bodies or minds. Trees, green roofs, and vegetated corridors deliver a suite of ecosystem services: they cool the surrounding air through evapotranspiration, intercept stormwater and reduce runoff, sequester atmospheric carbon, filter airborne pollutants, and provide habitat that supports urban biodiversity. As cities confront climate change, this green infrastructure is increasingly valued for its role in building environmental resilience, and municipalities worldwide have begun to treat tree canopy and vegetated cover as measurable civic assets. The economic and regulatory case for expanding urban greenery has grown accordingly.

Within this broad landscape of urban health and environment, there remains a distinct and less settled question about the mind rather than the body or the climate. This review therefore asks specifically whether exposure to urban green space reduces depression and anxiety, and synthesizes the evidence across observational, experimental, and genetically-informed designs to test the hypothesis that greater residential green space is associated with lower risk of these two mental-health outcomes.

## Abstract

Eight studies examining urban green space exposure and mental health outcomes consistently demonstrated protective associations with depression. A 0.1 increase in residential NDVI within 500m was associated with 15% lower odds of self-reported depression diagnosis[P. Hystad et al., 2019], while meta-analytic estimates indicated a 6.9% reduction in depression risk per 0.1 unit NDVI increase[Ziquan Liu et al., 2023]. Higher neighborhood green space levels were associated with significantly lower depression symptomology across multiple populations[K. Beyer et al., 2014]. Critically, a twin study using within-pair monozygotic comparisons found that the association between green space and depression persisted after controlling for genetic and shared environmental confounds[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015], providing strong evidence for a causal relationship.

Evidence for anxiety outcomes showed greater heterogeneity. Most studies reported protective associations, with one finding 19% lower odds of moderate anxiety per 0.1 NDVI increase[P. Hystad et al., 2019] and experimental studies demonstrating immediate improvements in state anxiety following brief green space exposure[I. Bray et al., 2021; I. Bray et al., 2022]. However, the twin study found no significant within-pair effect for anxiety when controlling for genetic factors and physical activity[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015]. This divergence may reflect differences between acute state anxiety benefits and long-term trait-level changes, or may indicate that physical activity mediates rather than confounds the green space-anxiety relationship. Overall, the evidence supports that urban green space exposure reduces depression risk, with more qualified support for anxiety reduction.

## Effects on Depression

All studies examining depression outcomes reported protective associations with green space exposure, though effect magnitudes varied by measurement approach and population.

| Study | Depression measure | Green space increment | Effect estimate | Statistical significance | Effect direction |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| P. Hystad et al., 2019 | Self-reported doctor diagnosis | 0.1 NDVI increase within 500m | OR=0.85 (95% CI: 0.76, 0.95) | p\<0.05 | Protective |
| P. Hystad et al., 2019 | Other depression measures | 0.1 NDVI increase within 500m | Protective but not statistically significant | Not significant | Protective |
| Ziquan Liu et al., 2023 | Depression and anxiety combined | 10% increase in green space | Merged OR=0.963 (95% CI: 0.948, 0.979) | p\<0.05 | Protective |

The Hystad et al. study found that a 0.1 increase in residential NDVI within 500m was associated with 15% lower odds of self-reported doctor diagnosis of depression[P. Hystad et al., 2019]. This effect size is comparable to the meta-analytic estimate from Liu et al., which found that a 0.1 unit increase in NDVI was associated with a 6.9% reduction in depression risk[Ziquan Liu et al., 2023]. Beyer et al. similarly reported that higher levels of neighborhood green space were associated with significantly lower levels of depression symptomology after controlling for multiple confounding factors[K. Beyer et al., 2014].

The twin study by Cohen-Cline et al. provided particularly strong evidence for a causal relationship between green space and depression, as the within-pair monozygotic twin design controlled for genetic and shared environmental confounds[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015]. The persistence of the association within genetically identical twins who experienced the same childhood environment suggests that green space exposure itself contributes to reduced depression risk[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015].

The Hystad et al. study found that a 0.1 increase in NDVI was associated with 19% lower odds of moderate anxiety on the GAD-7 scale[P. Hystad et al., 2019].

Experimental evidence from the systematic reviews by Bray et al. demonstrated that brief exposure to green environments (15-minute walks in forests or parks) produced immediate improvements in state anxiety compared to urban environments[I. Bray et al., 2021; I. Bray et al., 2022]. These acute effects on state anxiety were consistently observed across experimental studies[I. Bray et al., 2021].

The meta-analysis by Liu et al. found that a 10% increase in green space proportion was associated with a 3.7% reduction in combined depression and anxiety risk[Ziquan Liu et al., 2023].

## Proposed Mechanisms

The systematic reviews identified several pathways through which green space may influence mental health outcomes. Observational studies suggested that social interaction, physical activity, and mindfulness mediate the relationship between green space exposure and mental health[I. Bray et al., 2021; I. Bray et al., 2022]. The reviews proposed that the absence of noise and the restorative qualities of green spaces promote mindfulness and interrupt rumination, which in turn reduce the risk of anxiety disorders and depression[I. Bray et al., 2022].

Roe's review highlighted the ability of natural stimuli such as trees, water, and light patterns to promote "involuntary attention," allowing the brain to disengage and recover from cognitive fatigue[Jenny Roe, 2016]. Bray et al. noted that green environments encourage "effortless mindfulness" without requiring formal training, with one study finding that learning mindfulness in a green setting was particularly helpful for beginners[I. Bray et al., 2021].

## Synthesis

The evidence base consistently demonstrates protective associations between urban green space exposure and depression across diverse populations and study designs. The convergence of findings from cohort studies, ecological analyses, meta-analyses, and a genetically-informed twin study provides robust support for this relationship[P. Hystad et al., 2019; Ziquan Liu et al., 2023; K. Beyer et al., 2014; Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015; Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013].

For anxiety outcomes, the evidence is more nuanced. While most studies found protective associations[P. Hystad et al., 2019; K. Beyer et al., 2014; I. Bray et al., 2021; I. Bray et al., 2022; Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013], the twin study by Cohen-Cline et al. found no significant within-pair effect for anxiety when controlling for genetic and shared environmental confounds[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015]. This discrepancy may reflect different measurement approaches (trait anxiety assessed via questionnaires versus state anxiety measured experimentally) or temporal dimensions of the association. The experimental evidence from Bray et al. showed consistent immediate improvements in state anxiety following brief green space exposure[I. Bray et al., 2021; I. Bray et al., 2022], suggesting that acute benefits may be more reliably demonstrated than long-term trait-level changes.

The variation in effect magnitudes across studies (OR ranging from 0.81 to 0.96 per standardized green space increment) likely reflects differences in exposure assessment methods, outcome measures, population characteristics, and adjustment for confounders. Studies using self-reported doctor diagnoses[P. Hystad et al., 2019] versus standardized symptom scales[K. Beyer et al., 2014] versus treatment counts[Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013] assessed different points along the mental health spectrum, from clinical diagnoses to subclinical symptoms.

The Cohen-Cline twin study's null findings for anxiety and stress[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015] warrant careful interpretation. While within-pair monozygotic analyses control for genetic and shared environmental confounds, they may underestimate effects if twin pairs tend to live in areas with similar green space levels, reducing statistical power. Additionally, the study controlled for physical activity, which may be a mediator rather than a confounder in the green space-mental health pathway[Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015]. If green space reduces anxiety partly by facilitating physical activity, adjusting for this variable could attenuate or eliminate the observed association.

The geographic specificity of findings should also be considered. Studies from North America[P. Hystad et al., 2019; K. Beyer et al., 2014; Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015] and New Zealand[Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013] may not fully generalize to other urban contexts with different cultural relationships with nature, climate patterns, or urban design characteristics. However, the meta-analysis by Liu et al., which synthesized findings across multiple countries, demonstrated consistent protective effects[Ziquan Liu et al., 2023], supporting broader generalizability.

Measurement of green space exposure varied from satellite-derived NDVI[P. Hystad et al., 2019; Ziquan Liu et al., 2023; Hannah Cohen-Cline et al., 2015] to GIS-calculated proportions and distances[Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013], with different spatial scales (300m, 500m, 3km buffers). Nutsford et al. found that associations were stronger for green space within 3km than within 300m[Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013], suggesting that both nearby useable green space and observable neighborhood greenness contribute to mental health benefits[Daniel Nutsford et al., 2013]. This finding indicates that the mechanisms may include both direct use of green spaces and visual or ambient exposure to greenness in the broader neighborhood environment.
