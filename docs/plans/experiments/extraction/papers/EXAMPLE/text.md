# Sleep Restriction Impairs Overnight Consolidation of Declarative Memory

## Abstract

We tested whether a single night of partial sleep restriction impairs the overnight consolidation of newly learned word pairs. Adults encoded 60 word pairs, then either slept normally or were restricted to four hours in bed. Restricted sleepers recalled fewer pairs the next morning, and the deficit tracked reduced slow-wave sleep. The findings link a single restricted night to measurable next-day memory loss.

## Introduction

Sleep supports the consolidation of declarative memories formed during the day. Classic work shows that a full night of sleep after learning improves later recall relative to an equivalent waking interval. The mechanism is thought to involve the reactivation of hippocampal traces during slow-wave sleep, which stabilises them in neocortical stores. Much of this evidence, however, comes from total sleep deprivation, which is rare in ordinary life. The everyday exposure is partial restriction — a short night, not a missing one. Whether a single restricted night is enough to disrupt consolidation, and whether any deficit tracks the amount of slow-wave sleep obtained, remains unclear. We addressed this gap directly.

## Methods

Forty healthy adults (aged 18 to 30) learned 60 unrelated word pairs to a criterion of one perfect run. Participants were randomly assigned to a normal-sleep group (eight hours in bed) or a restricted-sleep group (four hours in bed). Sleep was recorded with polysomnography. The next morning, cued recall was tested: participants saw the first word of each pair and reported the second. The primary outcome was the number of pairs recalled out of 60. Slow-wave sleep minutes were extracted from the polysomnography record for each participant.

## Results

Restricted sleepers recalled fewer word pairs than normal sleepers the next morning (38.2 versus 47.6 pairs out of 60; d = 1.12, p < .001). The size of the memory deficit tracked the amount of slow-wave sleep obtained: across all participants, recall correlated with slow-wave sleep minutes (r = 0.54, p = .002). Restricted sleepers obtained 41% less slow-wave sleep than normal sleepers (52 versus 88 minutes). Encoding performance did not differ between groups at learning (p = .71), so the morning difference cannot be explained by weaker initial learning. A control test of simple reaction time showed no group difference the next morning (p = .48), indicating the effect was specific to memory rather than general alertness.

## Discussion

A single night of partial sleep restriction produced a large next-day deficit in declarative recall, and the deficit scaled with the loss of slow-wave sleep. This extends the total-deprivation literature to the far more common case of a short night. Because encoding was matched at learning, the deficit reflects impaired overnight consolidation rather than impaired acquisition. The specificity control argues against a general alertness account. A limitation is that recall was tested once, the morning after; whether the deficit persists or recovers over subsequent nights is unknown.
