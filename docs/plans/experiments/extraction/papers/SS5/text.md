# SS5 — Does a universal basic income reduce recipients labor supply?

**Review summary (Elicit):** Universal basic income does not significantly reduce recipients' labor supply.

**Papers extracted:** 8 of 8 retrieved (5 with full text). **Source:** Elicit systematic review — https://elicit.com/review/559b0291-469b-4dec-8f1e-2d9cf319a950

# Results

## Characteristics of included studies

The review identified 8 studies examining the relationship between universal basic income (UBI) or unconditional cash transfers and labor supply outcomes. Studies employed diverse methodological approaches including empirical analyses of existing programs, simulation models, and systematic reviews.

| Study | Full text retrieved? | Study type | Geographic context | Main finding |

The table demonstrates substantial methodological diversity, with 5 of 8 studies providing full-text access for detailed analysis. Studies span empirical analyses of real-world programs, theoretical simulations, and systematic evidence synthesis. Geographic contexts range from individual U.S. states to European welfare systems to developing countries, suggesting broad applicability of findings across institutional settings.

## Effects

Effects varied considerably across studies in both magnitude and direction, though most found either negligible or small effects on labor supply.

Three studies found effects contradicting the hypothesis that UBI significantly reduces labor supply [Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez et al., 2020; I. Marinescu, 2018], while two studies using neoclassical simulation models found UBI would increase labor market participation by eliminating unemployment traps [B. Gilroy et al., 2012; B. Gilroy et al., 2013]. The largest observed effect was a 0.7% annual labor market contraction in Alaska [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019], while the most precise estimate of the income effect suggested a marginal propensity to earn of -0.14 [J. Gromadzki, 2021], meaning recipients reduce earnings by only 14 cents for every additional dollar of unearned income received.

Several studies identified heterogeneous effects across demographic groups. Bibler et al. found that women, particularly younger, lower-wage workers with young children, reduced hours worked following cash transfers, while men showed increased employment probability [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019]. The systematic review by de Paz-Báñez et al. noted functional reductions in labor supply among children, the elderly, the sick, those with disabilities, women with young children, and young people continuing their studies, but emphasized these reductions did not decrease overall supply as they were offset by increased supply from other community members [Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez et al., 2020].

The temporal dimension emerged as important across multiple studies. Ren's analysis emphasized that reductions in labor supply were "small and short-run" [Bofan Ren, 2026], suggesting effects may dissipate over time. Conversely, the Polish child benefit study by Gromadzki found persistently small effects even after accounting for adjustment periods [J. Gromadzki, 2021].

## Synthesis

The apparent heterogeneity in findings—ranging from labor supply increases to small decreases—can be reconciled through several key distinctions in study contexts, methodologies, and populations examined.

**Context-specific effects and the unemployment trap mechanism**

Studies diverge primarily on whether the cash transfer operates in a context with pre-existing welfare traps. The German simulation studies found that basic income increases labor market participation [B. Gilroy et al., 2012; B. Gilroy et al., 2013] specifically because it eliminates the unemployment trap inherent in means-tested benefits, where individuals lose benefits upon earning income. In these contexts, unconditional transfers create positive labor supply effects by removing the high effective marginal tax rates faced by welfare recipients. This mechanism is distinct from the pure income effect examined in contexts without strong welfare traps, explaining why these studies find increases while others find small decreases or null effects.

The systematic review by de Paz-Báñez et al. similarly found global increases in labor supply [Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez et al., 2020], with 38 studies showing no evidence of significant reduction. This finding reflects that in many real-world implementations, UBI replaces or supplements existing welfare systems, generating net positive effects through trap elimination that outweigh pure income effects.

**Magnitude of transfer and income effects**

The studies finding small negative effects consistently examine transfers representing meaningful but not transformative income shares. Marinescu's review established that a 10% income increase leads to only a 1% labor supply decrease [I. Marinescu, 2018], while Gromadzki found a marginal propensity to earn of -0.14 [J. Gromadzki, 2021]. These estimates are remarkably consistent and suggest income effects operate within a narrow bandwidth regardless of context.

Bibler et al.'s finding of a 0.7% annual labor market contraction [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019] aligns with these magnitudes when the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend is expressed as a percentage of household income. The heterogeneity appears in which groups reduce labor supply: women with young children showed the largest responses [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019], suggesting the income effect enables choices around caregiving that were previously constrained. This is not a failure of UBI but a feature allowing individuals to align labor supply with caregiving responsibilities.

**Methodological hierarchy and real-world implementation**

The studies employing theoretical simulation models (Gilroy et al., Lee) predict increases or minimal effects [B. Gilroy et al., 2012; B. Gilroy et al., 2013; Seungju Lee, 2024], while empirical analyses of implemented programs (Ren, Bibler, Gromadzki) find small decreases or mixed effects [Bofan Ren, 2026; Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019; J. Gromadzki, 2021]. This divergence likely reflects that simulations model the unemployment trap mechanism explicitly, while empirical studies capture both trap elimination and income effects simultaneously, with the net effect depending on the baseline welfare system's generosity.

Marinescu's review, which synthesized evidence from multiple empirical programs including the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend and casino dividends, found predominantly null effects [I. Marinescu, 2018]. The Alaska program showed no impact on employment-to-population ratios [I. Marinescu, 2018], yet Bibler et al.'s more granular analysis of the same program found small reductions when examining hours worked among employed women [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019]. This suggests that while extensive margin effects (whether to work) are negligible, intensive margin effects (hours worked) may be small but detectable, particularly among groups with caregiving responsibilities.

**Time horizons and adjustment dynamics**

Ren emphasized that reductions are "small and short-run" [Bofan Ren, 2026], suggesting labor supply effects may dissipate as households adjust to permanent income changes. The Finnish basic income experiment, analyzed in Ren's study, showed minimal long-term employment effects despite initial small reductions. This temporal pattern indicates that initial reductions may reflect optimal adjustments to new budget constraints rather than permanent labor force withdrawal. Over longer periods, households may increase labor supply as they make human capital investments enabled by income security, though the studies reviewed here primarily capture short- to medium-term effects.

**Synthesis of labor supply effects across contexts**

For working-age adults without caregiving constraints in contexts without strong welfare traps, the evidence consistently indicates negligible to small negative effects, with income elasticities around -0.10 to -0.15 [I. Marinescu, 2018; J. Gromadzki, 2021]. For individuals previously caught in unemployment traps, unconditional transfers increase labor supply by eliminating benefit withdrawal rates [B. Gilroy et al., 2012; B. Gilroy et al., 2013]. For women with young children and other groups with caregiving responsibilities, transfers enable reductions in market work that appear to reflect preferences rather than disincentives [Andrew J. Bibler et al., 2019; Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez et al., 2020]. The systematic review finding of global labor supply increases [Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez et al., 2020] likely reflects that most implemented programs occurred in contexts where unemployment trap elimination dominated income effects, while controlled experiments in contexts without pre-existing traps (like Alaska) revealed small pure income effects.

The policy implication is that labor supply effects depend critically on baseline welfare system design. In generous welfare states with high effective marginal tax rates on benefit recipients, UBI likely increases labor supply. In contexts with minimal safety nets, pure income effects may reduce labor supply by 1-2% but enable welfare-improving adjustments in time allocation. Neither scenario suggests UBI creates mass labor force withdrawal.

## Per-paper findings

Each entry lists the paper's single most important finding as extracted by Elicit, its classified effect direction, and a verbatim supporting quote copied from the paper.

### J. Gromadzki, 2021 — Labor supply effects of a universal cash transfer

- **Main finding:** The introduction of a universal child benefit in Poland results in very small labor supply effects, with a $100 increase in monthly benefits leading to only a $14 decrease in monthly earnings, indicating that recipients' labor supply is not significantly reduced.
- **Effect direction:** null or negligible
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "I find very small labor supply effects on both the intensive and extensive margin. The estimated labor supply effects of receiving the unconditional transfer are small. For every extra 100 dollars in monthly child benefit transfer households receive, they reduce their monthly earnings by 14 dollars. The marginal propensity to earn out of unearned income was equal to -0.14, similar to the estimates obtained by Cesarini et al. (2017) for Sweden. At the individual level, the introduction of the child benefit had almost no impact on parental labor supply at the extensive and the intensive margin."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4025162

### B. Gilroy, 2012 — Center for International Economics Working Paper Series Basic Income and Labor Supply: the German Case Basic Income and Labor Supply: the German Case

- **Main finding:** A basic income increases the participation rate in the labor market and encourages current employees to increase their labor supply, thereby expanding employment.
- **Effect direction:** supports the hypothesis
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "the basic income increases the participation rate in the labor market"

### B. Gilroy, 2013 — Basic Income and Labour Supply: The German Case

- **Main finding:** The implementation of a universal basic income increases the participation rate in the labor market by abolishing the unemployment trap, despite some employees potentially reducing their labor supply.
- **Effect direction:** supports the hypothesis
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "We show that, even in the neoclassical labour supply model without intrinsic work motivation, an unconditional basic income increases the participation rate in the labour market."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1515/bis-2012-0009

### I. Marinescu, 2018 — No Strings Attached: The Behavioral Effects of U.S. Unconditional Cash Transfer Programs

- **Main finding:** The main finding is that unconditional cash transfers have either no significant effect or a very small effect on labor supply, with a 10% income increase leading to only a 1% decrease in labor supply.
- **Effect direction:** contradicts the hypothesis
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "Many studies find no statistically significant effect of an unconditional cash transfer on the probability of working. In the studies that do find an effect on labor supply, the effect is small: a 10% income increase induced by an unconditional cash transfer decreases labor supply by about 1%. The evidence does not suggest an average worker will drop out of the labor force when provided with unconditional cash, even when the transfer is large. Overall, the programs analyzed suggest either no effect on labor market supply or a slight reduction in work and earnings. The casino dividend had no impact on the labor supply of participants. The Alaska dividend has no impact on the employment-to-population ratio in Alaska compared to control states. The research finds a $100 increase in income leads to an $11 decrease in earnings based on lottery winners and negative income tax experiment participants, or no decrease at all in the case of the casino payments."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.3386/W24337

### Manuela A. de Paz-Báñez, 2020 — Is There Empirical Evidence on How the Implementation of a Universal Basic Income (UBI) Affects Labour Supply? A Systematic Review

- **Main finding:** The implementation of a Universal Basic Income (UBI) does not lead to a significant reduction in labor supply; instead, it increases labor supply globally among adults, men and women, young and old, with some minor exceptions.
- **Effect direction:** contradicts the hypothesis
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "Despite a detailed search, we have not found any evidence of a significant reduction in labour supply. Instead, we found evidence that labour supply increases globally among adults, men and women, young and old, and the existence of some insignificant and functional reductions to the system such as a decrease in workers from the following categories: Children, the elderly, the sick, those with disabilities, women with young children to look after, or young people who continued studying."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.3390/SU12229459

### Andrew J. Bibler, 2019 — Short-Term Labor Responses to Unconditional Cash Transfers

- **Main finding:** The main finding is that an increase in the universal cash transfer (PFD) induces both a positive labor demand shock and a negative labor supply response, resulting in a 1.6% labor market contraction in the months following the disbursement, or a 0.7% contraction on an annual basis.
- **Effect direction:** mixed
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "We estimate that an additional $1,000 in the per-person PFD leads to an average decrease of 1.25 hours of work per week in the months following the PFD disbursement in the sample of employed women."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.2139/SSRN.3357230

### Bofan Ren, 2026 — Universal Basic Income and the Labour Market:A Microeconomic Analysis with Evidence from InternationalExperiments

- **Main finding:** The main finding is that Universal Basic Income (UBI) leads to a small and short-run reduction in labor supply, consistent with income effects predicted by the labor-leisure model, but this effect is small and temporary.
- **Effect direction:** mixed
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "The empirical results indicate that UBI leads to a small and short-run reduction in labour supply, consistent with income effects predicted by the labour-leisure model."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.54254/2754-1169/2026.ld31635

### Seungju Lee, 2024 — Examining the potential impact of universal basic income on labor supply: Focusing on the South Korean models

- **Main finding:** The main finding is that UBI exerts minimal negative effects on labor supply among full-time and part-time workers and encourages labor force entry among unemployed individuals, suggesting it supports labor participation without substantial disincentives for employment.
- **Effect direction:** null or negligible
- **Supporting quote (verbatim):** "The results suggest that UBI exerts minimal negative effects on labor supply among full‐time and part‐time workers and appears to encourage labor force entry among unemployed individuals."
- **Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/ijsw.12715
