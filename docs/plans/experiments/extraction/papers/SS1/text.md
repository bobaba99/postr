# SS1 — Does higher social media use cause lower well-being in adolescents?

*Systematic review synthesized in Elicit ([review](https://elicit.com/review/552effa6-06a4-4cc3-9f13-9946cc03ea9c), session `552effa6-06a4-4cc3-9f13-9946cc03ea9c`). Field: social science. 8 papers passed full-text screening and extraction.*

> Note: The Elicit API exposed the per-paper extraction (main finding, verbatim supporting quote, direction of effect) but did not surface a separate free-text synthesis/report markdown body. The Results synthesis below is composed strictly from the returned per-paper extraction — no findings, quotes, papers, or numbers have been added beyond what Elicit returned.

## Results

Across the eight studies extracted, the dominant pattern is that longitudinal and within-person designs find **little evidence that higher social media use causes lower well-being in adolescents**, in contrast to the negative associations often reported in cross-sectional work. Two UK longitudinal analyses of the UK Household Longitudinal Study concluded there was little evidence that more time on social media was associated with later mental health problems, with one reporting no adjusted association between time on social media and poorer mental health two years later (n=2603; b=0.21 [95% CI -0.43 to 0.84]; p=0.52). A narrative review of longitudinal studies similarly found limited robust evidence that the *frequency* of use directly impacts well-being, while noting that *passive* use was linked to greater depressed mood and *disordered* use to lower life satisfaction. A within-person longitudinal study of depressive symptoms reversed the assumed causal arrow: depressive symptoms predicted small increases in active social media use, with no evidence of the reverse. An experience-sampling typology found that, on average, momentary well-being did not change with time spent on social media, and that person-specific effects were heterogeneous — 45% of adolescents experienced no change, 28% declines, and 26% increases in well-being. A neuroimaging longitudinal study reported associations between higher use and brain-structure changes that did not survive statistical correction. The clearest countervailing signal comes from a large JAMA Pediatrics study, which found a **U-shaped** association between after-school social media use and well-being — both the heaviest use and abstinence were associated with lower well-being — with the highest-use adolescents showing markedly greater odds of low well-being than moderate users. Taken together, the extracted evidence does not support a simple causal claim that more social media use lowers adolescent well-being; effects are small, frequently null at the within-person level, sometimes reverse-directional, and appear to be non-linear and person-specific.

## Per-paper findings

### 1. The longitudinal effect of social media use on adolescent mental health in the UK: findings from the UK Longitudinal Household Study.
R. Plackett, J. Sheringham, J. Dykxhoorn — *The Lancet*, 2022. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(22)02281-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(22)02281-4)

**Main finding.** There is little evidence to suggest a causal relationship between the use of social media and mental health issues 2 years later in UK adolescents.

**Supporting quote.** "In adjusted analysis, no association between time spent on social media and poorer mental health was identified (n=2603; b=0·21 [95% CI -0·43 to 0·84]; p=0·52)."

### 2. Social Media Use and Adolescent Well-Being: A Narrative Review of Longitudinal Studies
Jenna Course-Choi, L. Hammond — *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*, 2020. [https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2020.0020](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2020.0020)

**Main finding.** The main finding is that there is limited robust evidence to suggest that frequency of social media use directly impacts adolescent well-being, but passive use is linked to greater depressed mood and disordered use is associated with lower life satisfaction.

**Supporting quote.** "The papers reviewed provide limited robust evidence that frequency of social media use impacts adolescent well-being. Most studies found no direct effects, contrary to the researchers' hypotheses and individual cross-sectional studies, which have suggested a negative relationship between frequency of social media use and well-being."

### 3. Longitudinal associations between social media use, mental well-being and structural brain development across adolescence
M. Achterberg, A. Becht, Renske van der Cruijsen, I. H. van de Groep, J. Spaans, E. Klapwijk, E. Crone — *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience*, 2021. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2022.101088](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2022.101088)

**Main finding.** The study found that higher social media use is associated with specific changes in brain structure, but these associations did not survive statistical correction, indicating a nuanced relationship between social media use and well-being that does not directly support the notion that higher social media use causes lower well-being.

**Supporting quote.** "Not mentioned (the abstract does not directly state that higher social media use causes lower well-being in adolescents)"

### 4. The Longitudinal Impact of Social Media Use on UK Adolescents' Mental Health: Longitudinal Observational Study

**Main finding.** There was little evidence to suggest that more time spent on social media is associated with later mental health problems in UK adolescents.

**Supporting quote.** "There was little evidence to suggest that more time spent on social media was associated with later mental health problems in UK adolescents."

### 5. The longitudinal impact of social media use on adolescent mental health in the UK
R. Plackett, J. Sheringham, J. Dykxhoorn — *European Journal of Public Health*, 2022. [https://doi.org/10.1093/eurpub/ckac129.054](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurpub/ckac129.054)

**Main finding.** This study found little evidence to suggest that more time spent on social media is associated with later mental health problems in UK adolescents.

**Supporting quote.** "This study shows the importance of longitudinal evidence, as we found there was little evidence to suggest a causal relationship between social media use and mental health issues two years later. This longitudinal study found little evidence to suggest that more time spent on social media is associated with later mental health problems in UK adolescents."

**Direction of effect.** null or negligible

### 6. Social Media Use and Depressive Symptoms—A Longitudinal Study from Early to Late Adolescence
Kati Puukko, L. Hietajärvi, Erika Maksniemi, K. Alho, K. Salmela‐Aro — *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 2020. [https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165921](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165921)

**Main finding.** Depressive symptoms predicted small increases in active social media use during both early and late adolescence, but there was no evidence that social media use led to increased depressive symptoms.

**Supporting quote.** "The results indicate that depressive symptoms predicted small increases in active social media use during both early and late adolescence, whereas no evidence of the reverse relationship was found. The results provide support for the growing notion that the previously reported direct links between social media use and depressive symptoms might be exaggerated. Our results provide little evidence that active, socially-oriented social media use and depressive symptoms develop together in adolescence at the within-person level. The present results provide some small support for the view that adolescents' social media use might increase when they experience greater depressive symptoms. The results contradict previous claims that social media use leads to greater depressive symptoms."

### 7. Social Media Use and Adolescents’ Well-Being: Developing a Typology of Person-Specific Effect Patterns
Ine Beyens, Pouwels Jl, van Driel, L. Keijsers, P. Valkenburg — *Communication Research*, 2020. [https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502211038196](https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502211038196)

**Main finding.** On average, the time adolescents spent using social media in an active private, passive private, or passive public way did not affect their momentary well-being, with 45% experiencing no changes, 28% experiencing declines, and 26% experiencing increases in well-being.

**Supporting quote.** "On average, adolescents' momentary levels of well-being were unrelated to the time they had spent on each of the three types of social media use in the hour before. While adolescents who spent more time using social media than their peers reported lower levels of well-being than their peers (i.e., significant between-person associations), on average, adolescents' momentary well-being did not change as they spent more time using social media (i.e., nonsignificant average within-person associations)."

**Direction of effect.** negative

### 8. Social Media Use and Well-Being Across Adolescent Development.
Ben Singh, Mason Zhou, Rachel Curtis, Carol Maher, D. Dumuid — *JAMA pediatrics*, 2026. [https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.5619](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.5619)

**Main finding.** The study found a U-shaped association between after-school social media use and adolescent well-being, with both heavy use and abstinence being associated with lower well-being, varying by age and sex.

**Supporting quote.** "Compared with moderate users, adolescents with the highest use had greater odds of low well-being (grades 7-9, girls: odds ratio [OR], 3.13 [95% CI, 2.88-3.39]; boys: OR, 2.25 [95% CI, 1.86-2.72])."
