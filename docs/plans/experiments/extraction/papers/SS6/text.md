# SS6 — Does bilingualism improve executive function in children?

## Results

Across the eight studies extracted, the evidence is **mixed**. Seven of the eight are individual empirical studies that report a bilingual advantage, but crucially that advantage is **domain-specific rather than global**: it shows up on tasks that require managing conflicting attentional demands (interference suppression, conflict resolution, cognitive flexibility) and does *not* appear on tasks requiring pure response inhibition or impulse control. Multiple studies make this restriction explicit — bilingual children performed significantly better on conflict/Stroop-type tasks "with no difference between groups on the other tasks," were better "in conditions based on inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility but there was no significant difference between groups in response suppression," and showed the advantage "for tasks that appear to call for managing conflicting attentional demands (Conflict tasks)" while showing "no advantage on impulse-control (Delay tasks)." One study found the advantage held in low-income minority children on control tasks but not representation tasks, and another found it emerging as early as toddlerhood on the Stroop task alone. One study added electrophysiological (ERP) evidence and framed bilingualism as a "brain-training" that improves executive control.

The single most important paper is the countervailing one: a **systematic review and meta-analysis** (Gunnerud et al., 2020, *Psychological Bulletin*) that pooled this literature and found the bilingual advantage in overall executive function to be **significant but marginal (g = 0.06)**, accompanied by indications of **publication bias** and **large unexplained heterogeneity** in effect sizes — concluding that the pooled evidence "gives little support for a bilingual advantage on overall EF." Taken together, the extracted evidence does not support a strong or global claim that bilingualism improves executive function in children: the effect that survives meta-analysis is very small and confounded by publication bias, and the positive individual studies are confined to specific conflict-monitoring/interference-suppression tasks rather than executive function as a whole.

## Per-paper findings

### 1. Is bilingualism related to a cognitive advantage in children? A systematic review and meta-analysis.
Hilde Lowell Gunnerud, Dieuwer ten Braak, Elin Reikerås, Enrica Donolato, M. Melby‐Lervåg — *Psychological bulletin*, 2020. [https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000301](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000301)

**Main finding.** The meta-analysis found a significant but marginal bilingual advantage in overall executive function in children, with a small effect size, but gives little support for a strong bilingual advantage due to publication bias and heterogeneity in effect sizes.

**Supporting quote.** "The bilingual advantage in overall EF was significant, albeit marginal (g = 0.06), and there were indications of publication bias. The EF components of cold inhibition, switching, and monitoring expressed significant bilingual advantages, but monitoring and cold inhibition were affected by publication bias. Thus, given the small mean effect size and small-study effects, this meta-analysis gives little support for a bilingual advantage on overall EF. Still, also after the moderator analysis, there was a large heterogeneity of true effects and a large amount of unexplained heterogeneity in the effect sizes."

**Direction of effect.** mixed

### 2. The development of two types of inhibitory control in monolingual and bilingual children
M. Martin-Rhee, E. Bialystok — *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition*, 2008. [https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728907003227](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728907003227)

**Main finding.** Bilingual children have an advantage in tasks requiring control over attention to competing cues (interference suppression) but not in tasks requiring control over competing responses (response inhibition).

**Supporting quote.** "Previous research has shown that bilingual children excel in tasks requiring inhibitory control to ignore a misleading perceptual cue."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 3. Bilingual experience and executive functioning in young children.
Stephanie M. Carlson, A. Meltzoff — *Developmental Science*, 2008. [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2008.00675.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2008.00675.x)

**Main finding.** Native bilingual children performed significantly better on executive function tasks, particularly those requiring conflict resolution and attention management, compared to monolingual and immersion groups, even after controlling for socio-economic and verbal ability differences.

**Supporting quote.** "After statistically controlling for these factors and age, native bilingual children performed significantly better on the executive function battery than both other groups. The relative advantage was significant for tasks that appear to call for managing conflicting attentional demands (Conflict tasks); there was no advantage on impulse-control (Delay tasks)."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 4. Components of Executive Control with Advantages for Bilingual Children in Two Cultures
E. Bialystok, M. Viswanathan — *Cognition*, 2009. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2009.06.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2009.06.014)

**Main finding.** Bilingual children outperform monolingual children in tasks requiring inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility, but not in response suppression, indicating that bilingualism enhances these specific components of executive control.

**Supporting quote.** "The bilingual children in both settings were faster than monolinguals in conditions based on inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility but there was no significant difference between groups in response suppression or on a control condition that did not involve executive control."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 5. The effects of bilingualism on toddlers' executive functioning
D. Poulin-Dubois, Agnès Blaye, Julie Coutya, E. Bialystok — *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, 2010. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2010.10.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2010.10.009)

**Main finding.** Native bilingual children performed significantly better than monolingual children on the Stroop task, indicating an early emergence of bilingual advantages in executive control.

**Supporting quote.** "Native bilingual children performed significantly better than monolingual children on the Stroop task, with no difference between groups on the other tasks, confirming the specificity of bilingual effects to conflict tasks reported in older children."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 6. Executive functions and inhibitory control in multilingual children: evidence from second-language learners, bilinguals, and trilinguals.
Gregory J. Poarch, Janet G. van Hell — *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, 2012. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2012.06.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2012.06.013)

**Main finding.** Bilinguals and trilinguals showed enhanced conflict resolution compared to monolinguals and second-language learners, indicating improved executive function.

**Supporting quote.** "bilinguals and trilinguals showing enhanced conflict resolution over monolinguals and marginally so over second-language learners."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 7. Bilingualism Enriches the Poor: Enhanced Cognitive Control in Low-Income Minority Children
Pascale M. J. Engel de Abreu, Anabela Cruz-Santos, Carlos J. Tourinho, Romain Martin, E. Bialystok — *Psychology Science*, 2012. [https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443836](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443836)

**Main finding.** Bilingual children from low-income backgrounds performed significantly better than monolingual children in cognitive control tasks, but not in representation tasks, indicating that bilingualism enhances executive functioning related to control.

**Supporting quote.** "Whereas there were no group differences in representation, the bilinguals performed significantly better than did the monolinguals in control."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis

### 8. Behavioral and Electrophysiological Differences in Executive Control between Monolingual and Bilingual Children
R. Barac, Sylvain Moreno, E. Bialystok — *Child Development*, 2016. [https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12538](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12538)

**Main finding.** Bilingual children outperform monolingual children in tasks requiring interference suppression and complex response inhibition, with electrophysiological evidence supporting these behavioral differences as indicative of enhanced executive control development.

**Supporting quote.** "Bilingual children outperformed monolinguals on interference suppression (ANT) and complex response inhibition (go/no-go task). On the go/no-go task, ERPs showed larger P3 amplitudes and shorter N2 and P3 latencies for bilingual children than for monolinguals. The results support the conclusion that bilingualism provides a powerful form of 'brain-training' that improves children's development of executive control."

**Direction of effect.** supports the hypothesis
